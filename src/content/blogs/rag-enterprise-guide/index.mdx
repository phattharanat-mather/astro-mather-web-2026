Large language models are impressive — until they confidently answer a question about your proprietary product line with completely made-up information. That's where Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) changes the equation.

## What RAG Actually Does

A standard LLM generates answers from patterns learned during training. RAG adds a retrieval step: before the model responds, it pulls relevant documents from your own data sources — internal wikis, PDFs, CRM notes, support tickets — and uses those as grounding context.

The result is an AI that answers from your knowledge base, not from generic internet data.

## Why Enterprises Are Adopting It Fast

Three reasons dominate enterprise conversations about RAG:

**Accuracy.** Grounded answers reduce hallucinations dramatically. When the model cites a passage from your actual documentation, it's far less likely to invent details.

**No retraining required.** Fine-tuning a model on your data is expensive and slow. RAG lets you swap in fresh knowledge by updating your document index — no GPU clusters needed.

**Auditability.** Because the source documents are surfaced alongside the answer, compliance teams can verify where information came from.

## Common RAG Architectures

Most enterprise deployments follow one of two patterns:

- **Naive RAG** — embed documents into a vector database (Pinecone, Weaviate, pgvector), retrieve the top-k chunks at query time, pass them to the LLM. Fast to ship, good starting point.
- **Advanced RAG** — adds query rewriting, re-ranking, and hybrid search (keyword + semantic). Better accuracy for complex or ambiguous questions.

## Where RAG Falls Short

RAG is not a silver bullet. It struggles when your documents are poorly structured, when questions require reasoning across many documents at once, or when the retrieval step returns irrelevant chunks. Garbage in, garbage out still applies.

The fix is usually better document chunking strategy and query-time reranking — not a bigger model.
