Most organizations underestimate what bad data actually costs them. It's not just wrong reports — it's wasted engineering hours, failed AI projects, and decisions made on a foundation of sand.

## The Numbers Are Worse Than You Think

Industry estimates consistently put the cost of poor data quality at 15–25% of revenue for large enterprises. The causes are mundane: duplicate records, inconsistent formats, missing fields, stale values that were never updated after a business process changed.

The damage compounds over time. A CRM with 30% duplicate contacts doesn't just produce bad marketing metrics — it corrupts any model you train on it.

## Where Bad Data Hides

The most dangerous data quality problems are the ones nobody notices:

- **Silent nulls** — fields that appear populated but contain placeholder values ("N/A", "0", "unknown") that get treated as real data downstream
- **Schema drift** — upstream systems change a field's meaning or format without telling anyone; pipelines keep running, but the semantics are wrong
- **Survivorship bias in historical data** — records for churned customers, discontinued products, or closed accounts are deleted instead of archived, poisoning trend analysis

## A Practical Fix: Data Quality at the Source

Fixing data quality in the warehouse after the fact is expensive and slow. The better approach is pushing validation upstream — to the systems that create records.

Concrete steps that actually work:

1. **Define non-negotiable fields** per entity type and enforce them at the API or form layer, not just in ETL
2. **Instrument your pipelines** with data quality checks (Great Expectations, dbt tests) that fail loudly rather than silently pass bad data through
3. **Assign data stewards** — technical ownership of data quality produces better outcomes than governance committees alone

## AI Makes This Urgent

If you're planning an AI initiative, data quality is not a precondition you can defer. Models trained on dirty data don't generalize — they amplify noise. Cleaning data after a model underperforms costs more than preventing the problem up front.
